## Four items reviewed specifically

**Q4 — order of adding acid, indicator, and base in a titration**

**Suggested teacher response:** There is no single universal order for every titration. For the
procedure described in Q4, where acid is added to a base, the sensible order is **base → indicator
→ add acid slowly**. The reverse arrangement, **acid → indicator → add base slowly**, can also be
valid when the acid is the measured solution in the flask and the base is the titrant. The item
should state which solution is in the flask and which is added from the burette. Also correct the
brief: its stated answer, “acid → indicator → base,” conflicts with Q4 and its answer key, which
currently select “base → indicator → acid.”

**Recommendation:** Revise Q4 so that the apparatus and titrant are unambiguous; do not claim that
one sequence is the only valid titration setup.

---

**Q7 — “the pH at the end-point is always exactly 7”**

**Suggested teacher response:** The distinction between strong and weak acids and bases is taught
in Sri Lankan Grade 11 Science; it is not restricted to A/L Chemistry. Grade 11 students are also
taught that a salt may be acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the strengths of the acid and base
that formed it. Therefore, the idea that every neutralisation mixture is not necessarily pH 7 is
within Grade 11 scope at a conceptual level.

However, the question should use **equivalence point** (the chemically equivalent amounts) rather
than **end-point** (the observed indicator colour change). Even in a strong-acid/strong-base
titration, an indicator's observed end-point is not necessarily exactly pH 7. A better statement is:
“At the equivalence point of every acid–base neutralisation, the solution is always pH 7.” The
correct response is then that this is true for a strong acid–strong base combination, but not for
all strong/weak combinations.

**Recommendation:** Keep the concept but revise “end-point” to “equivalence point” throughout the
item and scoring key.

---

**Q12 — “add acid to water, never water to acid”**

**Suggested teacher response:** This is a separate laboratory-safety rule. It is not the same idea
as the order used to observe a titration end-point. When concentrated acid is diluted, adding water
to acid can cause rapid local heating, boiling, or splashing. In a titration, the order matters
mainly because the indicator must be present before the end-point is approached and because the
titrant must be delivered gradually. These are different reasons and should not be treated as one
misconception without a broader, explicitly justified construct.

**Recommendation:** Keep Q12 only as a safety item. Do not score it as evidence for the
titration-order misconception `CHEM_001`; replace it with another titration-specific item if three
items are required for that code.

---

**Q13 — weak/strong neutralisation and the final pH**

**Suggested teacher response:** The review brief does not match the post-test item. The brief says
weak ethanoic acid + strong base, but Q13 in §4 uses **weak ammonia base + strong hydrochloric acid**.
The pre-test P13 uses ethanoic acid + sodium hydroxide.

Grade 11 students are taught the conceptual result: the salt formed can give an acidic, basic, or
neutral solution depending on the strengths of the parent acid and base. They are not expected to
use the detailed A/L treatment of salt hydrolysis, equilibrium constants, or a numerical pH
calculation. The present reason, “the salt reacts slightly with water,” introduces the hydrolysis
mechanism unnecessarily.

For Q13, the expected Grade 11 conclusion is that ammonium chloride from a weak base and strong
acid gives a slightly acidic solution. For P13, sodium ethanoate from a weak acid and strong base
gives a slightly basic solution.

**Recommendation:** Keep both items but replace the hydrolysis wording with a Grade 11-level reason,
for example: “A salt formed from a weak base and a strong acid gives an acidic solution” (Q13), and
the corresponding weak-acid/strong-base statement for P13.

## Curriculum basis for this provisional view

- Sri Lanka Grade 11 Science, Chapter 7, teaches complete/partial ionisation and classifies acids
  and bases as strong or weak (textbook pp. 191 and 194).
- The same chapter states that salts can be acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the strengths of
  the acid and base that form them (pp. 197 and 199).
- The Sri Lanka A/L Chemistry syllabus contains the detailed treatment: salt hydrolysis, pH of salt
  solutions, acid–base titrations, equivalence-point calculations, and titration curves.

Official references:

- [Grade 11 Science — Chapter 7: Acids, Bases and Salts](https://e-thaksalawa.moe.gov.lk/lcms/pluginfile.php/42714/mod_resource/content/2/Chapter%2007.pdf)
- [Sri Lanka G.C.E. A/L Chemistry syllabus](https://nie.lk/pdffiles/tg/AL_Syl%20Chemistry.pdf)
